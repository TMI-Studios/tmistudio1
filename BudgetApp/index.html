South African salary budget examples

Wondering how to budget a R5,000, R10,000 or R15,000 salary? These illustrative monthly scenarios show how different incomes might be allocated. Actual costs depend on your location, household size, housing, transport and responsibilities.

How to budget a R5,000 take-home salary

Start with essentials and costs you cannot avoid. At this income, a strict 50/30/20 split may not be realistic if you pay rent or support relatives.

Example if you share housing: transport R700, bank fees R50, airtime/data R200, shared housing contribution R1,200, groceries R1,000, utilities R250 and family support R300. Total listed costs: R3,700. Remaining: R1,300.
Step 1: Protect essentials. Make sure food, safe housing, transport to work and essential bills are covered before spending on wants.
Step 2: Give the remainder a job. If the R1,300 is genuinely available, an example is R500 emergency savings, R500 irregular costs or debt, and R300 personal spending. Change this if you have overdue bills or high-interest debt.
Step 3: Review housing costs. If rent leaves too little for food and transport, explore safer shared housing or a family arrangement if practical. Moving is not possible or safe for everyone.

All figures are examples only. Check actual costs where you live.

How to budget a R10,000 take-home salary

A budget can use a percentage guide, but the amounts need to match your real commitments.

Example monthly plan: housing R2,500; groceries R1,500; transport R1,000; utilities R500; bank fees R80; airtime/data R250; insurance/medical R500; family support R1,000; debt repayments R500. Total: R7,830. Remaining: R2,170.
Step 1: Set aside essentials and commitments first. Include recurring bills and support you have committed to send.
Step 2: Build a starter emergency fund. If you have no cash buffer, consider saving a small first milestone you can reach while keeping up with required payments.
Step 3: Tackle expensive debt. After minimum payments, paying extra toward high-interest debt may be more useful than investing while the debt grows.
Step 4: Plan personal spending. Give hair, clothing, outings and subscriptions a realistic limit rather than relying on whatever happens to remain.

Example costs total R7,830; the R2,170 remainder is before any costs not listed.

How to budget a R15,000 take-home salary

A higher income can create room to save, but fixed commitments and debt can absorb it quickly.

Example monthly plan: housing R3,500; groceries R2,000; transport R1,500; utilities/internet R700; bank fees R100; airtime/data R300; insurance/medical R800; family support R1,000; debt repayments R1,000. Total: R10,900. Remaining: R4,100.
Step 1: Keep lifestyle costs visible. Include car costs, subscriptions, school expenses, annual bills and irregular spending so the remainder isn't overstated.
Step 2: Choose a priority. Depending on your circumstances, focus on a starter emergency fund, overdue essentials, high-interest debt or a planned upcoming expense.
Step 3: Invest only with a plan. Consider time horizon, fees, risk and whether you can leave the money invested. Investments can fall in value; returns aren't guaranteed.

How to budget when you support family (black tax)

Family support can be a meaningful responsibility, but it needs a realistic place in the budget. Include it as a planned commitment so you can see what is genuinely affordable.

  1. Write down recurring support. For example, a monthly contribution toward groceries or school costs.
  2. Agree on a sustainable amount. If possible, communicate what you can send and when you can review it.
  3. Keep an emergency buffer in view. If you cannot cover your own essentials, revisit the amount and look for other support options where possible.
  4. Make room for irregular requests. Decide what you can offer for emergencies without assuming every extra request can be met.
Boundary script: "I can contribute R___ each month, and I'll send it on ___. I can't promise extra money outside that amount, but I'll let you know if my situation changes."
How to compare South African bank fees

There is no single cheapest bank for every customer. Monthly account fees, card purchases, cash withdrawals, transfers and other transaction charges can change the total.

  1. Find the bank's latest official pricing guide for your exact account type.
  2. List the transactions you normally make in a month, including cash withdrawals and transfers.
  3. Compare the estimated monthly total, not only the monthly account fee.
  4. Check whether salary deposits, debit orders and payment needs work with the account before switching.
  5. Confirm current fees directly with the bank before making a decision.

Important: Prices and account features change. This guide intentionally does not publish unverified fee figures or promise a fixed annual saving.

Monthly budget checklist

  • Start with take-home income, not gross salary.
  • Include recurring bills, debt minimums and family support.
  • Plan for irregular costs such as school needs, repairs and annual fees.
  • Check the budget again when prices or income change.
  • Use savings targets that fit your actual situation; percentages are guides, not rules.

Budget calculator: common questions

Should I budget using my gross or take-home salary?

Use your take-home salary: the money you receive after deductions. If you budget using gross salary, you may overestimate the money available for rent, groceries, bills and savings.

What if my monthly expenses are higher than my salary?

The calculator will show a negative balance. Review the figures and check for expenses that can safely be reduced. Prioritise essential living costs and required minimum debt payments. If you are struggling with overdue debt or essential bills, consider contacting a reputable, appropriately qualified support provider.

Does this calculator save my financial information?

This calculator runs in your browser and does not send your budget figures to a calculator server. It does not save your entries between visits. If you choose to copy your budget summary, it is copied to your device's clipboard.

Is the 50/30/20 budget rule suitable for everyone?

No. The 50/30/20 rule is only a guideline. If essentials take up most of your income, you may need a different allocation. Cover essential needs and required payments before trying to follow a fixed percentage split.

Your money. Your actual life.

A useful budget is one you can realistically follow. Include the bills you pay, the people you support, and the irregular expenses that always seem to arrive at the worst time. Start with your actual numbers and adjust as your circumstances change.
